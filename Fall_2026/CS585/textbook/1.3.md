## 1.3.1 Packet Switching

- <u>Packets</u>: A subset of a long message (a small chunk of it)
- Data travels through communication links and packet switches:
    > " if a source end system or a packet switch is sending a packet of L 
    > bits over a link with transmission rate R bits/sec, then the time to 
    > transmit the packet is L / R seconds"
- Two types of packet swithces:
    1. Routers
    2. Link-Layer Switches
### Store-and-Forward Transimssion

- <u>Store-And-Forward Transmission</u>: the packet switch must recieve the 
  entire packet, before it can transmit any outbound packets
- "Most packet switches use store-and-forward transmission at the inputs to the
  links."
- Packets get buffered, while awaiting the full message
- Affects on transmission speeds:
    > "At time L/R seconds, since the router has just received the entire 
    > packet, it can begin to transmit the packet onto the outbound link 
    > towards the destination; at time 2L/R, the router has transmitted the 
    > entire packet, and the entire packet has been received by the 
    > destination. Thus, the total delay is 2L/R. If the"switch instead 
    > forwarded bits as soon as they arrive (without first receiving the 
    > entire packet), then the total delay would be L/R since bits are not 
    > held up at the router."
- Formula: "sending one packet from source to destination over a path 
  consisting of N links each of rate R (thus, there are N-1 routers between 
  source and destination)":

$$
d_{\text{end-to-end}} = N \frac{L}{R}
$$

### Queuing Delays and Packet Loss

- Each packet switch has multiple links (where it can send to?)
- <u>Output Buffer (output queue)</u>: Packet switches have an output buffer 
  per link (stores the packets that the router is about to send into that link)
- <u>Queuing Delays</u>: I'm a packet, I want to be sent into a link on a 
  switch, but there is already stuff in the outptu queue for this link, i.e. I
  must wait my turn
    - <u>Packet Loss</u>: The queue is finite, if it is full, my packet will 
      have nowhere to go, some packet will have to be dropped or I will have to
      be dropped
      
### Forwarding Tables and Routing Protocols

- How does a switch decide where to forward a packet in the Internet?:
    - Each end system has a unique IP address, destination IP addresses are 
      included in the packet's header
- <u>Forwarding Table</u>: maps destination adresses to a router's outbound 
  links
- Routing protocls are used to automatically set forwarding tables of routers
    - Ex: use shortest path to configure forwarding tables

## 1.3.2 Circuit Switching

- Two methods of moving data through a network of links and switches: (1) 
  circuit switching, and (2) packet switching
- <u>Circuit Switching</u>: the resources needed along the path of 
  communication are reserved for the duration of the communication
    - Analogy: reservations at a restaurant -> more setup time, but less in 
      queue the at the resetauraunt
    - Ex: a telephone call requires a stable connection between sender and 
      reciever (a **circuit**)
- <u>Circuit</u>: A connection between sender and receiver maintained for the 
  duration of communication
    - Have a gaurunteed throuput rate
- Still an issue of congestion: packets still have to wait in a buffers at the
  sending side of transmission links if links are busy
  
### Multiplexing in Circuit-Switched Networks

- Two types of circuit link implementations:
    - <u>Frequency-Division Multiplexing</u> (FDM) 
    - <u>Time Division Multiplexing</u> (TDM)
> "With FDM, each circuit continuously gets a fraction of the bandwidth. With 
  TDM, each circuit gets all of the bandwidth periodically during brief 
  intervals of time (that is, during slots)"
- In TDM: transmission rate $=$ frame rate $\times$ number of bits per slot
- Cons:
    - <u>Silent Periods</u>: times when dedicated circuits are idle
    - establishing end-to-end circuits is complex/expensive
- Pro:
    - Transmission time is independent of number of links

### Packet Switching Versus Circuit Switching

- Packet switching is more popular

- Packet Switching:
    - Pros:
        - "Better sharing of transmission capacity"
        - Simple -> less costly to implement
    - Cons:
        - Variable end-to-end delays -> worse for real-time services
- Circuit Switching:
    - Pros:
        - Better for real time services
    - Cons:
        - Wasteful

## 1.3.3 A Network of Networks

- ISP: Internet Service Provider
- Access ISP: local internet service provider
- <u>Regional ISP</u>: access ISPs connect to some regional ISP
- <u>Tier-1 ISPs</u>: 'global' provider:
    - Approximately 12 exist: AT&T, Sprint, Level 3 Communications, NTT, etc.
- Each access ISP pays regional ISP, which pays tier-1 ISP:
    - It is possible for an access ISP to get direct tier-1 ISP connection 
      (pays them directly)
- <u>Points of Presence</u> (PoPs): A group of one or more routers at the same 
  location, customer's pay to connect to these
- <u>Multi-home</u>: An ISP (except tier-1) can connect to two or more ISPs
- <u>Peer</u>: Two neary ISPs at the same level can directly connect their 
  networks together, so that traffic between them is direct
- <u>Internet Exchange Point</u> (IXP): a meeting point where multiple ISPs 
  can peer together
- <u>Content-Provider Networks</u>: 
    - EX: Google has private TCP/IP network which spans the globe, and only 
      carries traffic to/from Google servers
    - Google peers with lower-tier ISPS through direct connection or IXP 
      connection (in order to not pay as much to the tier-1s), but still pays 
      the tier 1s for areas which it can not get arround in such a manner
- Today's Internet (Network Structure 5): "consisting of access ISPs, regional 
  ISPs, tier-1 ISPs, PoPs, multi-homing, peering, and IXPs" + Content-Provider 
  Networks
